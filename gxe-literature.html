Placenta xQTL Atlas

11 Literature context: G×ppBMI findings

Placenta xQTL Atlas — Module 07 G×E (ppBMI)
Review date: 2026-10-12

11.0.1 Scope

This review places the current placenta genotype × maternal pre-pregnancy BMI (G×ppBMI) results in the context of prior work on maternal adiposity, placental molecular biology, and BMI-dependent genetic regulation. The project results are treated as the primary evidence; external literature is used to assess biological plausibility and novelty.

A critical distinction is maintained throughout: most prior placental studies test maternal BMI or obesity main effects on placental molecular traits, whereas the present analysis asks whether genetic effects on placental molecular phenotypes vary as a function of ppBMI. A BMI-associated placental gene is therefore not automatically a G×BMI gene, and vice versa.

11.0.2 Summary of the current ppBMI G×E results

The supplied Module-07 aggregates contain 444,582 ancestry-specific Tier-1 feature tests for the exact ppBMI exposure label. At q < 0.05, there are 2,067 significant ancestry-specific rows representing 2,064 unique molecular features. Splicing accounts for 1,782 of these rows (86.2%), with a particularly large EUR contribution.

Cross-ancestry synthesis is much more restrictive. Only two molecular features are significant in both EAS and EUR, and neither uses the same ancestry-specific lead variant. The features map to TAF6 and MRAS. These should be described as recurrent cross-ancestry molecular phenotypes rather than variant-level replications. Across the broader Tier-1 meta table, 1,285 features pass the feature-level ACAT q-value threshold.

Tier 2 contains 35,090 genetically prioritized phenotypes. Although 33,949 (96.7%) have q_meta < 0.05, most are supported by only one ancestry and the set was pre-enriched by external genetic prioritization. Requiring two ancestries, concordant interaction direction, nominal P < 0.05 in both ancestries, and P_heterogeneity >= 0.05 reduces the set to 664 molecular features. That stricter subset is more useful for biological prioritization than the raw Tier-2 q-value count.

11.0.3 1. Maternal adiposity measurably alters placental molecular state

There is strong prior evidence that maternal BMI and obesity affect the human placental transcriptome, epigenome, and metabolic environment.

A 2024 genome-wide RNA-seq study of 450 placentas from the Gen3G cohort found that higher maternal BMI in early pregnancy was associated with lower placental EPYC expression and higher IGFBP6, CHRDL1, and CXCL13 expression after multiple-testing correction [1]. This establishes that maternal BMI can be reflected in the term placental transcriptome even after adjustment for major pregnancy covariates.

Earlier placental transcriptome studies reached broadly consistent pathway-level conclusions. In a small case-control study, maternal pre-pregnancy obesity was associated with 72 differentially regulated placental genes and with inflammation/immune, lipid-metabolism, cancer, and angiogenesis pathways [2]. A separate RNA-seq study of placentas from lean versus obese pregnancies found 288 differentially expressed genes and evidence for altered lipid metabolism, angiogenesis, hormone activity, cytokine activity, oxidative stress, and lipotoxicity [3]. These studies are not G×E analyses, but they support ppBMI as a biologically meaningful placental environment.

Placental epigenetic studies provide a parallel line of evidence. In the NICHD Fetal Growth Studies, pre-pregnancy BMI was associated with placental DNA methylation at loci including EGFL7 and VEZT; both ppBMI and methylation at the EGFL7-associated CpG were negatively correlated with placental EGFL7 expression [4]. Thus maternal adiposity is capable of engaging multiple molecular layers of the placenta.

11.0.3.1 Implication for the present atlas

The present G×ppBMI results extend this literature from mean molecular differences by BMI to genotype-dependent molecular responses across BMI. They are therefore conceptually complementary rather than redundant.

11.0.4 2. BMI-dependent regulatory effects are biologically established

Human G×BMI regulatory mapping has precedent outside placenta.

Glastonbury and colleagues analyzed RNA-seq from 856 female twins across multiple tissues and identified cis G×BMI effects on gene expression, with significant interactions concentrated in adipose tissue and enriched in adipocyte enhancers and metabolic/inflammatory programs [5]. This study is important for two reasons: it shows that BMI can act as a continuous regulatory environment, and it demonstrates that G×BMI effects can be strongly tissue-specific.

More recently, Signer and colleagues used an interaction framework to map BMI-dynamic eQTLs across tissues and showed that BMI-by-genotype models can identify context-dependent regulatory relationships influenced by cell type, putative enhancer activity, and inflammatory cytokines [6]. Their results further support the general premise that a SNP’s regulatory effect is not necessarily fixed across an individual’s physiological environment.

11.0.4.1 Relevance to placenta

Neither study establishes the specific placental interactions observed here. Instead, they validate the regulatory model class: BMI is a plausible continuous context that can modify genetic control of gene expression. The placenta is especially appropriate for this question because it is directly exposed to the maternal metabolic milieu while retaining a fetal genotype.

11.0.5 3. The strong splicing signal has emerging biological precedent

The most striking feature of the ppBMI Tier-1 results is the predominance of splicing interactions. This pattern should be treated as a major hypothesis, not as already-established biology, because direct human placental genotype×ppBMI splicing studies remain sparse.

Two recent studies nevertheless make the observation biologically plausible.

First, a 2026 rat time-series placental RNA-seq study reported that maternal high-fat diet disrupted placental spliceosome pathways during mid-to-late gestation, including altered expression of multiple spliceosome genes [7]. Importantly, the authors observed spliceosome disruption even in obesity-resistant animals, suggesting that metabolic/dietary context and adiposity phenotype need not be interchangeable.

Second, a 2026 human study found widespread differential pre-mRNA splicing in placentas from pregnancies affected by gestational diabetes mellitus and implicated SRSF10 as a regulator of a subset of the missplicing [8]. Gestational diabetes is not ppBMI, and this study did not test genotype interactions, but it independently demonstrates that placental RNA processing is responsive to a metabolic pregnancy environment.

11.0.5.1 Interpretation

The atlas result therefore fits an emerging model in which placental splicing is metabolically responsive. The novel contribution is the suggestion that the magnitude of cis-genetic control over splicing itself depends on maternal ppBMI. Replication in an independent placenta cohort would be particularly valuable because the number and correlation structure of splice events can magnify modality-level counts.

11.0.6 4. Tier-1 candidate loci

11.0.6.1 CFLAR

CFLAR is among the strongest/recurrent Tier-1 signals, with significant ppBMI interactions across molecular modalities and ancestries. The strongest current signal is an EAS alternative-polyadenylation phenotype.

CFLAR encodes FLICE-inhibitory protein (FLIP), an inhibitor of caspase-8. Human placental work has demonstrated long and short FLIP isoforms in early and late gestation placenta and in trophoblast cells, with expression patterns varying by cell lineage and differentiation state [9]. The study interpreted FLIP as an important component of trophoblast survival.

This provides direct placental biological support for CFLAR, although not for a maternal-BMI interaction specifically. A ppBMI-dependent regulatory effect at CFLAR is mechanistically plausible in a placenta exposed to obesity-related inflammatory and oxidative-stress pathways, but functional validation is needed.

Evidence assessment: strong placental relevance; G×ppBMI mechanism novel.

11.0.6.2 PRLR

PRLR has multiple significant Tier-1 splicing features, particularly in EUR. The prolactin receptor is directly expressed in human fetal membranes, decidua, placenta, and trophoblast compartments [10]. The broader placental lactogenic axis is also sensitive to maternal obesity: obesity has been associated with disrupted placental lactogen chromosomal architecture and more than a 40% decrease in term human placental lactogen RNA in one study [11].

These observations make PRLR one of the more compelling biologically contextualized G×ppBMI candidates. They do not establish that PRLR splicing is caused by obesity or that the current interacting variant is functional.

Evidence assessment: direct placental receptor evidence plus obesity- sensitive lactogenic context; specific G×ppBMI splicing effect novel.

11.0.6.3 SKP2

SKP2 shows significant G×ppBMI evidence across more than one molecular phenotype in the results. A 2026 placental study showed that SKP2 is highly expressed in placenta and co-expressed in syncytiotrophoblast precursor, syncytiotrophoblast, and cytotrophoblast populations; it also identified a RhoBTB1/CUL3-dependent mechanism regulating SKP2 stability [12].

This is unusually direct recent placental evidence for a project hit. The connection to maternal BMI remains untested experimentally.

Evidence assessment: strong contemporary placental evidence; ppBMI- dependent genetic regulation novel.

11.0.6.4 SGMS1

SGMS1, encoding sphingomyelin synthase 1, has recurrent EUR splicing signals in the current Tier-1 results. Maternal obesity is well established to alter placental lipid metabolism and create a lipotoxic/oxidative-stress environment [3]. This gives SGMS1 a coherent pathway-level rationale, but the literature reviewed here does not provide direct evidence that SGMS1 itself is a placental maternal-BMI target.

Evidence assessment: strong pathway plausibility; direct locus-level placental G×ppBMI evidence currently limited.

11.0.6.5 SLC25A36

SLC25A36, a mitochondrial carrier, is another recurrent Tier-1 signal. Maternal obesity has been linked to altered placental lipid handling, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial biology [3]. As with SGMS1, this supports the biological neighborhood rather than the specific gene–variant interaction.

Evidence assessment: mitochondrial/metabolic plausibility; direct placental G×ppBMI evidence limited.

11.0.6.6 SYT8

SYT8 is one of the most recurrent Tier-1 genes by number of significant features and appears across ancestry-specific results. Outside placenta, SYT8 has been linked to glucose-regulated insulin-secretory biology, making it a plausible metabolic-context candidate. However, the literature reviewed for this report did not identify strong direct evidence connecting SYT8 to maternal obesity in human placenta.

Evidence assessment: interesting metabolic candidate; placental G×ppBMI role appears novel.

11.0.6.7 PBRM1

PBRM1 appears across significant molecular features and ancestries in the project. PBRM1 is a chromatin-remodeling component with reproductive biology evidence, but direct human placental maternal-BMI evidence is currently thin. Its value here is therefore primarily statistical/internal until validated.

Evidence assessment: mechanistically plausible chromatin regulator; placental ppBMI specificity novel.

11.0.6.8 TAF6 and MRAS: cross-ancestry molecular recurrence

The only two Tier-1 molecular features significant in both EAS and EUR map to TAF6 and MRAS. This is important evidence of cross-ancestry recurrence at the phenotype level, but it is not same-variant replication: both features have different ancestry-specific lead variants. MRAS also has different interaction directions at the two distinct leads.

These loci should therefore be prioritized for locus-level conditional or joint fine-mapping rather than described as replicated allele-specific G×ppBMI effects.

11.0.7 5. Tier-2 candidates and how to use them

Tier 2 is enriched by design because it begins with loci supported by colocalization and/or TWAS-based criteria. Its very high rate of q_meta < 0.05 therefore cannot be compared directly with Tier-1 discovery yield.

A stricter two-ancestry sensitivity subset retains 664 features. Recurrent genes in this set include CCDC107, ACTG1, CUTA, CNGA1, DAXX, BTN3A2, CLEC1A, and RPS18, among others. These are useful targets for integrated prioritization, especially when the same gene also has independent fine-mapping, colocalization/colocBoost, TWAS, or cell-type evidence.

The literature review should not assign equal biological weight to every Tier-2 q-value. In particular:

  • one-ancestry Tier-2 rows are discovery/follow-up evidence, not replication;
  • the selected variant is the best meta-analysis variant within a pre-prioritized phenotype;
  • molecular phenotypes from the same gene are correlated; and
  • for one-ancestry rows, the final normal-theory meta z-test can be more extreme than the finite-df ancestry nominal p-value.

The two-ancestry, directionally concordant subset is therefore the preferable Tier-2 layer for headline biological interpretation.

11.0.8 6. Ancestry heterogeneity is a result, not merely a nuisance

The project shows major ancestry differences in the number and distribution of G×ppBMI discoveries. This can arise from several nonexclusive sources: sample-size/power differences, allele-frequency differences, genotype support within the exposure distribution, LD structure, distinct ancestry-specific lead variants, and true biological heterogeneity.

The current data do not justify concluding that ppBMI-dependent regulation is intrinsically more prevalent in one ancestry. The scarcity of same-lead cross-ancestry features makes that especially important. Ancestry-specific results should remain primary, with cross-ancestry summaries serving as a synthesis layer.

11.0.9 7. Integrated biological interpretation

Taken together, the results and prior literature support four working hypotheses.

  1. Maternal adiposity is a genuine placental molecular environment. Human transcriptomic and epigenetic studies consistently demonstrate placental responses to maternal BMI and obesity [1-4].
  2. Genetic regulation can be BMI-dependent. Human G×BMI eQTL studies show that adiposity modifies regulatory variant effects in a strongly tissue/context-specific manner [5,6].
  3. RNA processing may be an important placental metabolic-response layer. The splicing predominance in this atlas is novel, but recent placental metabolic-stress studies independently implicate spliceosome and pre-mRNA-splicing regulation [7,8].
  4. The strongest candidates are those with orthogonal evidence. Genes such as CFLAR, PRLR, and SKP2 combine strong internal interaction evidence with direct placental biology, whereas several other high-ranking loci remain attractive precisely because their placental G×ppBMI roles appear novel.

11.0.10 8. Recommended next analyses

For atlas-level prioritization, the most informative next step is to intersect the G×ppBMI results with independent evidence rather than rank on G×E q-value alone. In particular:

  • annotate Tier-1 and robust Tier-2 features with Module-05 credible sets;
  • intersect G×ppBMI genes with SuSiE-coloc and colocBoost loci;
  • overlay TWAS gene-level evidence;
  • use the cell-type interaction annotations to ask whether G×ppBMI loci are concentrated in specific placental cellular contexts;
  • distinguish exact-variant overlap from gene-only overlap;
  • perform locus plots for TAF6 and MRAS to understand the discordant ancestry-specific leads; and
  • where individual-level data permit, pursue joint main+interaction fine-mapping rather than treating the top marginal interaction variant as the causal variant.

11.0.11 Conclusions

The placenta ppBMI G×E atlas is consistent with a broader literature in which maternal adiposity reshapes placental molecular state and BMI modifies genetic regulatory effects in human tissues. Its most distinctive observation is the large burden of ppBMI-dependent splicing regulation. The literature provides emerging metabolic-splicing precedent but not direct replication, making this a potentially important novel contribution.

The strongest locus-level biological candidates should be prioritized on multiple lines of evidence rather than interaction significance alone. CFLAR, PRLR, and SKP2 have particularly clear independent placental support; TAF6 and MRAS merit focused cross-ancestry locus analysis; and recurrent splicing candidates such as SGMS1, SLC25A36, SYT8, ASPRV1, and others provide a broader discovery set for functional follow-up.
